
# Ship 30 for 30 — Transcript-Grounded Essay Skill

## Purpose

Turn an answer from The Lenny Growth Assistant into a useful, readable, approximately 1,250-word online essay about product management, startups, or growth.

Every essay must be grounded in the retrieved Lenny's Podcast transcript passages. Writing quality must never come at the expense of factual accuracy or source attribution.

## When to Use This Skill

Activate this skill when the user requests:
- A Ship 30 for 30 essay.
- A long-form online article based on podcast insights.
- A publishable product or growth essay from the current conversation.
- A structured essay with a strong hook, narrative progression, and practical takeaway.

Do not activate it for ordinary short answers, code requests, or unrelated tasks.

## Core Writing Principles

### 1. Start with a compelling hook

Open with a concrete problem, surprising observation, tension, or counterintuitive idea relevant to the reader.

The first few sentences should establish:
- Who the essay is for.
- What problem or question it explores.
- Why the reader should continue.

Prefer a specific situation over a generic opening such as "In today's fast-paced world."

Do not invent a personal experience, customer story, statistic, or quotation to make the opening more dramatic. If the transcripts provide a useful example, use it accurately and cite it.

### 2. Choose one clear direction

Before drafting, select one primary approach:

- Actionable: explain how to accomplish something.
- Analytical: examine evidence, patterns, or trade-offs.
- Aspirational: show what becomes possible and why it matters.
- Anthropological: explain why people or teams behave in a particular way.

Choose the direction that best answers the user's request and is supported by the retrieved material.

Avoid trying to cover every possible angle in one essay.

### 3. Build a consistent structure

Choose one organizing pattern before writing:

- A sequence of practical steps.
- A set of lessons.
- A list of mistakes.
- A problem–insight–solution narrative.
- A comparison of competing approaches.

Use consistent headings and progression. If the essay promises five lessons, deliver five distinct lessons.

Create a short outline before drafting. Each section must advance the main argument rather than repeat an earlier point.

### 4. Make every section useful

Each major section should contain some combination of:
- A clear claim or lesson.
- Transcript-supported evidence or an accurately attributed example.
- An explanation of why the idea matters.
- A practical implication for the reader.

Do not add filler merely to reach the requested word count. If the evidence is limited, explain the limitation instead of inventing additional lessons.

### 5. Optimize for online readability

Use:
- Descriptive, benefit-oriented headings.
- Short paragraphs with varied sentence lengths.
- Bullets or numbered lists when they improve clarity.
- Selective **bold emphasis** for important ideas.
- Transitions that make the argument easy to follow.

Avoid making every sentence a separate paragraph. Also avoid long blocks of dense prose.

Use a fast rate of revelation: each paragraph should introduce a new idea, example, implication, or step. Remove repeated explanations and unnecessary introductory material.

### 6. Use stories and examples responsibly

A concrete example can make an abstract idea memorable.

Prefer real examples present in the retrieved transcripts. Attribute the example to the correct guest and episode.

Clearly distinguish:
- What a guest explicitly said.
- What the transcript reasonably suggests.
- Your own synthesis or practical recommendation.

Never fabricate dialogue, personal experiences, company results, customer reactions, or causal relationships.

### 7. Make the takeaway actionable

The essay must leave the reader with a specific next action.

Where the evidence supports it, include a short checklist, experiment, decision framework, or sequence of steps.

The takeaway should be achievable and connected to the essay's main argument. Avoid vague conclusions such as "keep learning and never give up."

If the transcripts do not establish a particular tactic, label it as a suggested experiment rather than a proven podcast recommendation.

## Grounding and Citation Rules

The transcript knowledge base is the authoritative evidence source for podcast-related claims.

1. Use only the retrieved passages and relevant conversation context provided to the skill.
2. Cite claims about a guest's advice with the corresponding source label, such as [Source 2].
3. Ensure each source label matches the episode metadata supplied by retrieval.
4. Never attribute one guest's statement to another guest.
5. Do not invent direct quotations. Use quotation marks only when the exact wording is present in the source.
6. Do not fabricate episode titles, guest names, dates, metrics, or links.
7. Do not present general knowledge as a claim made by Lenny Rachitsky or a guest.
8. If the evidence is insufficient for a requested conclusion, say so and narrow the essay's scope.
9. If two sources disagree or address different contexts, preserve that distinction rather than forcing agreement.
10. Keep citations close to the claims they support.

Before drafting, identify the most relevant passages and map each major claim to its evidence. Do not use a source merely because it shares a keyword with the topic.

## Drafting Workflow

Follow these steps in order:

1. Interpret the user's topic, audience, and intended outcome.
2. Review the available transcript passages and identify their strongest supported insight.
3. Choose one writing direction and one consistent structure.
4. Write a one-sentence thesis describing the essay's central argument.
5. Draft a hook that makes the problem concrete.
6. Organize the body into distinct, logically ordered sections.
7. Support factual claims with accurate source citations.
8. Translate supported insights into practical implications.
9. End with a memorable conclusion and a specific reader action.
10. Review the complete draft for evidence, structure, clarity, and length.

If the current conversation does not contain enough relevant evidence, retrieve additional transcript passages before writing when retrieval is available. Never pretend that retrieval succeeded when it did not.

## Length and Output Format

Target approximately 1,250 words for the complete essay, including the introduction, body, and conclusion. Treat the target as a guide, not a reason to add filler.

Return the essay in Markdown with:
- A clear title.
- A strong opening hook.
- An introduction that establishes the problem and thesis.
- Descriptive section headings.
- Skimmable paragraphs and selective bullets.
- Transcript citations near supported claims.
- A specific, useful conclusion.

Provide a short source list after the essay when source metadata is available. Include episode title, guest, and date when provided by retrieval.

Do not include internal planning notes, hidden reasoning, or fabricated source references in the final essay.

## Final Quality Checklist

Before returning the essay, verify:

- [ ] The hook gives the reader a reason to continue.
- [ ] The intended audience and central argument are clear.
- [ ] The structure is consistent and easy to follow.
- [ ] Every section advances the argument.
- [ ] Claims attributed to guests are supported by retrieved passages.
- [ ] Citation labels match the correct episodes.
- [ ] Direct quotations preserve the source wording.
- [ ] Paragraphs, headings, and lists support skimming.
- [ ] The essay avoids repetition and unsupported claims.
- [ ] The conclusion contains a specific practical takeaway.
- [ ] The length is approximately 1,250 words.
- [ ] Source metadata is accurate and no sources have been invented.

Quality principle: Be interesting without exaggerating, useful without overclaiming, and concise without losing the evidence that makes the essay trustworthy.
